FlyRank ML Internship — Capstone Research Paper

The Gap Between Position and Clicks

Ranking web pages by how far their click-through rate falls short of what their search position should earn — and testing whether a model can find them better than a rule can.

By Noor Zahra Repo & notebooks →
POSITION TIER 1  →  TIER 5 · GOLD = THE GAP THIS PAPER HUNTS FOR
Abstract Search pages that rank in similar positions usually earn similar click-through rates, but some fall well short of their peers, quietly losing traffic no one is watching for. This paper scores every visible page in a 30,000-page FlyRank dataset by that gap, and compares a Random Forest model against a transparent hand-written baseline rule at finding the pages worth reviewing first. Using a client-holdout split to keep the test honest, the model's top-50 picks matched the true worst-performing pages twice as often as the baseline rule's did. The result is a ranked, reason-coded review queue — a starting point for a content editor's next look, not a verdict on any single page.
01

Problem framing

A content team can't manually review thousands of pages every week. Left unmanaged, the pages quietly underperforming their search position stay invisible until traffic loss is already severe. This work turns that blind spot into a short, ordered list: which pages to open first, and a plain-language reason why each one made the cut.

Unit of analysis: one content page. Output: a rank and a reason code. Action a human takes: review the page's title, snippet, or freshness against the reason given, and decide whether to act. Cost of a wrong call: low — this is a prioritized suggestion list, not an automated edit, so a false positive costs a few minutes of review, not a broken page.

Only pages with impressions_90d ≥ 500 and an average position between 1 and 20 are scored. Below that traffic floor, a click-through-rate comparison is mostly noise — a page with ten impressions can swing from 0% to 30% CTR on a single click.

Columns deliberately excluded as model inputs: ctr and clicks_90d (the ingredients of the label itself — using them would be circular), trend_direction and trend_pct (flagged as leakage risks in the internship's own data dictionary), and the client and content identifiers (used only to keep test data separate from training data, never as a prediction input).

03

Methodology

Target: ctr_gap — a page's own click-through rate minus the median click-through rate of other visible pages at the same position tier. It's built entirely from observed clicks and impressions, not a hand-defined rule.

Baseline. A transparent rule: flag a page if its CTR sits under half its position tier's median, and it has at least 500 impressions. Rank the flagged pages by traffic volume. Simple, explainable, and the bar the model has to clear.

Model. A Random Forest Regressor predicting ctr_gap from content and traffic signals: search volume, competition, word and character count, content age, freshness, engagement rate, scroll rate, AI-referral traffic share, content type, search intent, and position tier. Chosen over a linear model because these signals interact — a stale page mostly matters if it also carries real traffic — and a tree ensemble captures that without hand-built interaction terms.

Validation design. A client-holdout split: 80% of clients for training, 20% held out entirely, so no client's pages appear on both sides. Pages from the same client often share templates and content strategy, so a random row-level split would let the model partly memorize client style rather than learn something that generalizes to a brand-new client.

Leakage audit. The final feature list was checked against every banned column — confirmed clean. As a second check, the same model was re-run under a naive random row split: R² came out higher there (0.43 vs. 0.36) than under the honest client-holdout split, the direction you'd expect if leakage were inflating the numbers — which is why the client-holdout result, not the leaky one, is what this paper reports.

The model's top-50 picks overlap with the true worst-performing pages roughly twice as often as the baseline rule's do. The Spearman correlation (0.763) shows the model's ranking tracks the true ranking well beyond just the top 50, not only at the cutoff.

FIG. 2 — What the model leans on most. Traffic and engagement signals dominate over content properties like word count.

Session volume, log-transformed impressions, and engagement rate carry the most weight — pages with more real activity simply give the model more signal to work with. Content properties like word count and freshness matter, but far less. Error review showed most of the model's false positives sit just outside the true cutoff — close calls, not wild misses — while the pages it missed tend to have unusually low session counts for their traffic level, a signal not fully captured by the current feature set.

05

Limitations & honest framing

  • Single snapshot. One 90-day window, no time-based validation yet — whether this holds across seasons is untested.
  • A proxy, not ground truth. ctr_gap measures underperformance today relative to peers, not whether fixing a page will raise its CTR tomorrow.
  • Correlational, not causal. The model finds association between page traits and the gap — it cannot tell a fixable snippet problem apart from a genuine intent mismatch.
  • Small held-out test set. 821 test rows across a handful of clients after grouping; Precision@50 on that few pages is noisier than a larger test would give.
  • Coverage gap. Only pages clearing the traffic floor are scored — about 18,000 lower-traffic pages in this snapshot are outside this queue entirely.
Every number in this paper should be read as observed, directional, decision-support — a starting point for a human's next look, not a guarantee, and not a claim about how Google's ranking algorithm works.
06

Ranked recommendations

The final model was re-trained on all visible pages and used to score and rank the full queue. Every flagged page carries a plain-language reason code, so a reviewer never sees a bare number without knowing why it's on the list.

How an editor would use this tomorrow: open the top 20–50 rows, read the reason code, and check the page against it — is the title actually weak, or is the intent mismatched? Nothing here auto-publishes; a person reads and decides on every row. See the capstone notebook for the full 12,023-row queue and methodology behind each reason code.
